## 📝 Master Answer — Section A (450 words / 10 marks)

### Q. Discuss psychosocial changes during old age. (10 marks)

**Introduction**

Late adulthood (approximately 60 years and beyond) is a stage marked by significant psychosocial transitions — retirement, changing social roles, loss of loved ones, and an increasing awareness of mortality. Psychosocial development during this period involves adapting to these changes while maintaining a sense of self-worth, purpose, and connection to others. Erik Erikson's psychosocial theory provides the most influential framework for understanding this stage.

**Erikson's Stage 8: Ego Integrity vs Despair**

According to Erikson, the central psychosocial task of late adulthood is the development of **ego integrity** — a sense that one's life has been meaningful, coherent, and worth living. Older adults engage in **life review**, reflecting on past experiences, accomplishments, and regrets.

- **Ego Integrity**: Achieved when the person can look back on life with acceptance, even acknowledging mistakes, and feel that choices were appropriate given the circumstances. Associated with wisdom, contentment, and the ability to face death without fear.
- **Despair**: Occurs when the individual feels that life was wasted, key opportunities were missed, or that there is no longer time to change course. Associated with depression, bitterness, and fear of death.

**Retirement and Its Psychosocial Impact**

Retirement is one of the most significant transitions of late adulthood. While many welcome retirement as freedom from work pressure, it can also bring:
- Loss of professional identity and status
- Reduced social network (work colleagues)
- Financial adjustments
- Sense of purposelessness

Successful adjustment to retirement involves replacing work-based roles with meaningful activities — volunteering, grandparenting, hobbies, and community participation. The quality of retirement experience is influenced by financial security, health, marital satisfaction, and attitudes toward retirement.

**Widowhood and Bereavement**

Late adulthood is often associated with the loss of a spouse — one of the most stressful life events. Widowhood has different impacts based on gender: widows (women) often draw on stronger social support networks, while widowers (men) are at higher risk of social isolation, depression, and health decline following bereavement.

Kübler-Ross's stages of grief — denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance — are widely applied to understanding bereavement, though grief processes are highly individual.

**Social Relationships**

Despite social losses, most older adults maintain a smaller but more emotionally meaningful social network (Carstensen's Socioemotional Selectivity Theory). They prioritise deep, satisfying relationships over new social contacts. Grandparenting provides an important source of meaning and intergenerational connection.

**Conclusion**

Psychosocial development in late adulthood is shaped by the interplay of biological ageing, role transitions, and accumulated life experience. Successful ageing involves achieving ego integrity, maintaining purposeful activity, sustaining meaningful relationships, and approaching mortality with equanimity — qualities that psychosocial theorists describe as wisdom.

## 📝 Short Note — Ego Integrity vs Despair (100 words / 3 marks)

**Ego Integrity vs Despair** is Erikson's eighth and final psychosocial stage, occurring in late adulthood (60+ years). The central task is reflecting on one's life — if the individual can look back with acceptance and a sense that life was meaningful, they achieve **ego integrity** (associated with wisdom and peace). If they feel regret and believe their life was wasted, they experience **despair** (associated with depression and fear of death). Successful resolution of this conflict requires having navigated earlier stages healthily — fulfilling relationships, meaningful work, and generative contributions to society.
