### Answer (Section A — ~450 words):

The **Normal Probability Curve (NPC)** is a theoretical ideal. In practice, empirical frequency distributions frequently diverge from this ideal in two main ways: **skewness** and **kurtosis**.

**1. Skewness** (asymmetry of the distribution):

Skewness refers to the lack of symmetry in a frequency distribution. In a perfectly normal curve, the distribution is symmetric; when it is skewed, one tail is longer than the other.

*Positive Skew (Right Skew)*: The tail extends to the right. Most scores cluster at the lower end; a few extreme high scores pull the mean to the right of the median and mode. Mean > Median > Mode.

*Negative Skew (Left Skew)*: The tail extends to the left. Most scores cluster at the higher end; a few extreme low scores pull the mean to the left. Mean < Median < Mode.

**Diagrams**:
- Positive skew: peak on the left, long tail to the right
- Negative skew: peak on the right, long tail to the left

**2. Kurtosis** (peakedness of the distribution):

Kurtosis refers to how peaked or flat the distribution is compared to the normal curve.

*Leptokurtic*: More peaked than normal — scores are concentrated around the mean, with heavier tails. Kurtosis > 0 (excess kurtosis positive).

*Platykurtic*: Flatter than normal — scores are more evenly spread across the range. Kurtosis < 0.

*Mesokurtic*: Normal kurtosis — same as the normal distribution. Kurtosis = 0.

**Diagrams**: Leptokurtic curve is tall and narrow; platykurtic is flat and wide; mesokurtic matches the bell curve.

**Factors Causing Divergence from Normality:**

1. *Sampling error*: In small samples, chance fluctuations can produce skewed or non-normal distributions even when the population is normal.

2. *Nature of the trait*: Some psychological traits are genuinely skewed in the population (e.g., income, reaction time, frequency of specific behaviours).

3. *Extreme scores / Outliers*: A few extreme values can distort the distribution toward skewness.

4. *Ceiling and floor effects*: When a test is too easy (most people score near maximum — negative skew) or too difficult (most score near zero — positive skew).

5. *Selective sampling*: If the sample is not randomly drawn from the population, the distribution may be skewed (e.g., sampling only high-achieving students produces a negatively skewed distribution).

6. *Measurement error*: Systematic errors in measurement can produce asymmetric distributions.

7. *Natural limits*: Variables bounded at zero (e.g., reaction time, number of errors) tend to show positive skew.

## 🧠 Memory Aid

**Skewness direction rule**: The tail points in the direction of the skew. Positive skew = tail points right = mean is pulled right.

**Kurtosis types (PLM)**: **P**latykurtic (flat) · **L**eptokurtic (lofty/peaked) · **M**esokurtic (matching normal)
