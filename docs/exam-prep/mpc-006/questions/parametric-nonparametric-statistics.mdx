**Appeared in all 27 papers in some variant. Exact phrasings include:**

### Answer (Section A — ~450 words):

Statistics can be broadly classified into two types based on the assumptions they make about the underlying population distribution: parametric and non-parametric statistics.

**Parametric Statistics:**
Parametric statistics are procedures that require the specification of certain conditions or parameters about the population from which the sample is drawn. The term "parametric" refers to population parameters such as the mean and standard deviation.

*Assumptions of Parametric Statistics:*
1. *Normal distribution*: The data in the population must be normally distributed. The sampling distribution of the statistic must be known and is usually assumed to be normal.
2. *Interval or ratio scale of measurement*: Parametric tests require that the data be measured on an interval or ratio scale, ensuring equal intervals between values.
3. *Homogeneity of variance*: When comparing two or more groups, the variances within each group should be approximately equal (homoscedasticity).
4. *Independence of observations*: Observations should be independent of each other.
5. *Random sampling*: Scores must be obtained from a random sample.

*Common parametric tests*: t-test, Pearson's r, ANOVA, regression analysis.

**Non-parametric Statistics:**
Non-parametric statistics (also called distribution-free statistics) are procedures that do not assume a specific distribution for the population. They are used when the assumptions of parametric statistics cannot be met.

*Assumptions of Non-parametric Statistics:*
1. Data may be measured on nominal or ordinal scales.
2. No assumption about the shape of the population distribution.
3. Observations should be independent (for most tests).

*Advantages of Non-parametric Statistics:*
1. *No distribution assumption*: Can be used when the population distribution is unknown or clearly non-normal.
2. *Applicable to all scales*: Work with nominal and ordinal data where parametric tests are inappropriate.
3. *Small samples*: More appropriate for very small samples where normality cannot be verified.
4. *Robustness*: Less affected by outliers and extreme scores.
5. *Simplicity*: Computational procedures are often simpler, especially for small samples.
6. *Exact probability*: Provide exact probabilities even for very small sample sizes.

*Disadvantages of Non-parametric Statistics:*
1. *Less powerful*: When parametric assumptions are met, parametric tests are more statistically powerful (less likely to make Type II errors).
2. *Waste of information*: Ranking procedures (used in many non-parametric tests) discard the actual magnitude of differences, losing information.
3. *Tied ranks*: When many scores are tied, adjustments are required that complicate computation.
4. *Limited scope*: Fewer available tests for complex designs (e.g., factorial designs, interaction effects).
5. *Critical tables*: Require separate critical value tables for each sample size.

**When to Use Non-parametric Tests:**
Use non-parametric tests when: (a) data are measured on a nominal or ordinal scale; (b) the sample is very small; (c) the population distribution is clearly non-normal; (d) there are extreme outliers that would distort parametric results.

## 📝 Other Key Questions

### Differentiate between parametric and non-parametric statistics (Section B — ~250 words)

| Aspect | Parametric Statistics | Non-parametric Statistics |
|--------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| **Assumption** | Assumes normal distribution | No distribution assumption (distribution-free) |
| **Scale** | Interval or ratio | Nominal or ordinal |
| **Parameters** | Based on population parameters (μ, σ) | Not based on population parameters |
| **Sample size** | Usually requires larger samples | Can be used with small samples |
| **Statistical power** | Higher power (when assumptions met) | Lower power |
| **Examples** | t-test, ANOVA, Pearson's r | Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis, Chi-square, Spearman's rho |
| **Information use** | Uses actual values | Often uses ranks (loses magnitude information) |
| **Complexity** | More complex with large samples | Simpler computation |

The choice between the two depends on the scale of measurement, sample size, normality of data, and the specific research question being addressed.
