### Answer (Section A — ~450 words):

**Hypothesis Testing** is a statistical procedure for making rational decisions about whether a sample outcome provides sufficient evidence to reject a stated null hypothesis about the population. It forms the cornerstone of inferential statistics.

**What is a Hypothesis?**
A hypothesis is a tentative statement about the relationship between variables or the characteristics of a population. In statistical testing, two complementary hypotheses are stated:

**Null Hypothesis (H₀)**: States that there is no significant effect, no difference, or no relationship. It is the hypothesis that the researcher tries to reject. Example: "There is no significant difference in anxiety scores between group A and group B."

**Alternative Hypothesis (H₁)**: States that a significant effect, difference, or relationship exists. It is what the researcher hopes to support. Example: "Group A has significantly higher anxiety scores than group B."

**Steps in Hypothesis Testing:**

1. *State the null and alternative hypotheses*: Clearly define H₀ and H₁ in testable terms. Decide if the test is one-tailed (directional) or two-tailed (non-directional).

2. *Choose the appropriate statistical test*: Based on scale of measurement, sample size, and research design (e.g., t-test for comparing two means, ANOVA for three or more means).

3. *Set the level of significance (α)*: Typically α = 0.05 (5%) or α = 0.01 (1%). This is the probability of making a Type I error (rejecting a true null hypothesis).

4. *Determine the critical region*: Using the chosen significance level and degrees of freedom, find the critical value from the appropriate statistical table. The critical region is the area of the sampling distribution that leads to rejection of H₀.

5. *Collect data and compute the test statistic*: Compute the appropriate statistic (t, F, χ², z) from sample data.

6. *Make a decision*: Compare the obtained statistic with the critical value.
   - If obtained value ≥ critical value: Reject H₀ (result is statistically significant)
   - If obtained value < critical value: Retain H₀ (result is not significant)

7. *State the conclusion*: Interpret the statistical decision in terms of the original research question.

**One-tailed vs Two-tailed Tests:**
A **one-tailed test** is used when the direction of the effect is predicted (e.g., Group A scores will be *higher* than Group B). The entire critical region falls in one tail. A **two-tailed test** is used when no direction is predicted; the critical region is split equally between both tails, making it more conservative.

**Errors in Hypothesis Testing:**
- **Type I Error (α error)**: Rejecting H₀ when it is actually true. The probability of this error equals the significance level (α).
- **Type II Error (β error)**: Retaining H₀ when it is actually false. The probability is denoted β; the power of the test = 1 − β.

The two errors are inversely related: reducing Type I error (using a stricter α) increases the risk of Type II error.

## 📝 Other Key Questions

### One-tailed and two-tailed tests and their applications (Section B — ~250 words)

**One-tailed test**: Used when the researcher has a specific directional prediction based on prior theory or evidence. The critical region is entirely in one tail (left or right). Advantage: higher statistical power for detecting the predicted direction. Risk: If the effect is in the opposite direction, it will be missed entirely.

**Two-tailed test**: Used when the research question is non-directional ("Is there a difference?"). The critical region is divided equally between both tails (α/2 in each). More conservative but safer for exploratory research.

**Decision rule**: One-tailed test at α = 0.05 uses the same critical value as a two-tailed test at α = 0.10, making the one-tailed test more powerful (easier to reach significance) when the prediction is correct.
