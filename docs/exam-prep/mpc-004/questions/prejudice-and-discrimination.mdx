## 🔥 Most Repeated Question: Methods of Reducing Prejudice and Discrimination (14 times)

**Appeared in**: 2013-Dec, 2014-Dec, 2015-Dec, 2016-Dec, 2017-Dec, 2018-June, 2019-June, 2020-June, 2020-Dec, 2021-Dec, 2022-June, 2022-Dec, 2023-June, 2024-June

### Answer (Section A — ~450 words):

Prejudice refers to an unjustified negative attitude towards members of a group solely based on their group membership. Discrimination is the behavioural expression of prejudice — treating people differently based on group membership. Reducing these involves multiple strategies operating at individual, interpersonal, and societal levels.

**1. Contact Hypothesis (Allport, 1954)**
Gordon Allport proposed that bringing members of different groups together under the right conditions reduces prejudice. The four conditions for effective contact are: equal status between groups, cooperative rather than competitive interaction, institutional support (laws, norms favouring contact), and acquaintance potential (getting to know individuals). Research on desegregation studies supports this — superficial contact alone is insufficient but meaningful equal-status contact reduces stereotyping. Extended contact effects (knowing someone who has cross-group friendships) also reduce prejudice.

**2. Education and Awareness**
Multicultural education, diversity training, and critical thinking programmes reduce stereotypic thinking by exposing people to accurate counter-stereotypic information. Perspective-taking exercises — imagining oneself in the position of a member of a stigmatised group — improve empathy and reduce explicit prejudice. Jigsaw classroom technique (Aronson) creates interdependence across group lines, reducing intergroup bias.

**3. Legislation and Social Norms**
Laws prohibiting discrimination signal societal disapproval, reducing discriminatory behaviour even if they do not immediately change private attitudes. When discriminatory behaviour becomes costly and normatively unacceptable, attitudes eventually shift to align with behaviour (cognitive dissonance). Anti-discrimination laws in employment, housing, and public accommodation have demonstrably reduced overt discrimination.

**4. Media and Representation**
Positive, counter-stereotypic portrayals in media challenge existing stereotypes. When media consistently show members of stigmatised groups in high-status, competent roles, stereotype activation is weakened over time. Social media campaigns promoting intergroup contact and shared identities also reduce prejudice at scale.

**5. Cognitive Strategies and Recategorisation**
Social categorisation naturally produces in-group/out-group distinctions. Recategorisation involves promoting a superordinate identity (e.g., "we are all Indians") that subsumes smaller group identities, reducing in-group bias. Common in-group identity model (Gaertner & Dovidio) shows that when competing groups see themselves as one group, bias decreases. Decategorisation — treating individuals as unique persons rather than category members — also reduces stereotypic judgements.

**6. Intergroup Cooperation — Superordinate Goals**
Sherif's Robbers Cave experiment demonstrated that creating shared goals requiring cooperation across group lines effectively reduced hostility. Cooperative learning structures in classrooms and workplaces apply this principle practically.

In conclusion, no single method eliminates prejudice entirely. The most effective approach combines legislative measures, educational initiatives, meaningful intergroup contact, and media representation working simultaneously at cognitive, emotional, and structural levels.

## 📝 Other Key Questions

### Explain prejudice and discrimination — nature, manifestation and causal factors

**Answer (Section A — ~450 words):**

Prejudice is a negative attitude based on faulty generalisation directed at a group and its members. It has three components: cognitive (stereotypic beliefs about the group), affective (negative emotions such as fear or contempt), and behavioural (tendency to discriminate). Discrimination is the overt, behavioural component — treating people differently and disadvantageously based on group membership.

**Manifestations of Prejudice:**
Allport (1954) proposed a scale of prejudice ranging from antilocution (talking disparagingly about a group), avoidance (keeping distance), discrimination (differential treatment), physical attack, and extermination (genocide). In contemporary societies, overt prejudice has declined but subtle and aversive forms persist — symbolic racism, modern sexism, and microaggressions are examples where prejudice is expressed indirectly.

**Causal Factors:**

*Cognitive Causes*: Social categorisation is a basic cognitive mechanism that reduces information-processing demands by placing people into groups. However, it simultaneously creates in-group/out-group distinctions and illusory correlations (overestimating the association between a minority group and undesirable behaviour). Stereotypes — cognitive schemas about group characteristics — are activated automatically upon perceiving group membership.

*Motivational Causes*: Realistic group conflict theory (Sherif) holds that prejudice arises when groups compete for scarce resources (jobs, housing, status). Social identity theory (Tajfel and Turner, 1979) argues that people derive self-esteem from their group memberships; enhancing in-group status relative to out-groups boosts self-esteem, driving in-group favouritism and out-group derogation. Authoritarian personality (Adorno et al.) — a cluster of traits including conventionalism, submission to authority, and aggression towards out-groups — predicts higher prejudice levels.

*Sociocultural Causes*: Prejudice is transmitted through socialisation — family, peers, school, and media shape group attitudes early in life. Cultural norms that legitimise inequality (e.g., caste system, historical racism) institutionalise discriminatory practices and make prejudice normatively acceptable. Economic conditions such as unemployment and downward mobility intensify competition for resources and heighten prejudice.

**Consequences:**
Prejudice harms both targets (stereotype threat, reduced performance, psychological distress, health disparities) and perpetrators (distorted social reality, missed opportunities for positive intergroup relations). Structural discrimination produces cumulative disadvantage across generations.

In conclusion, prejudice and discrimination emerge from the interaction of cognitive tendencies, motivational needs for positive group identity, and sociocultural contexts that normalise intergroup hierarchy.

### Discuss the characteristics, development and maintenance of stereotypes

**Answer (Section B — ~250 words):**

Stereotypes are cognitive schemas containing generalised beliefs about the characteristics of members of a social group. They are the cognitive component of prejudice.

**Characteristics**: Stereotypes are overgeneralised, oversimplified, resistant to disconfirmation, shared within cultures, and often negative towards out-groups. They exhibit the out-group homogeneity effect — members of out-groups are perceived as more similar to each other than in-group members.

**Development**: Stereotypes develop through social learning (observing parents, peers, media), direct experience (salient encounters with group members), illusory correlations (overestimating co-occurrence of minority groups and negative events), and social categorisation processes that simplify the social world.

**Maintenance**: Several mechanisms maintain stereotypes despite disconfirming evidence. Subtyping — placing exceptions into a subcategory (e.g., "she's not like other women") — protects the overall stereotype. Confirmation bias leads people to notice and remember stereotype-consistent information while ignoring inconsistent instances. Social transmission reinforces stereotypes through language, humour, and media portrayals that consistently associate groups with particular traits.

**Reducing Stereotypes**: Counter-stereotypic information presented repeatedly, individualisation of out-group members, and perspective-taking exercises can gradually weaken stereotypic associations.
