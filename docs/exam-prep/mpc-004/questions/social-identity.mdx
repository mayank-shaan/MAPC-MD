## Key Question: Social Identity and Social Identity Theory

### Answer (Section A — ~450 words):

Social identity refers to that part of an individual's self-concept that derives from their membership in social groups — gender, nationality, religion, profession, family — along with the emotional significance attached to that membership. Tajfel (1981) defined social identity as "that part of an individual's self-concept which derives from his knowledge of his membership in a social group together with the value and emotional significance attached to that membership."

**Social Identity Theory (Tajfel and Turner, 1979):**
Tajfel and Turner proposed that three cognitive processes link group membership to self-esteem and intergroup behaviour:

**1. Social Categorisation**
People classify themselves and others into social categories (men/women, Indian/Pakistani, student/worker). Categorisation simplifies the social world, accentuates within-category similarities, and between-category differences (categorisation accentuation effect). Merely being categorised into a group is sufficient to produce in-group favouritism.

**2. Social Identification**
People adopt the identity of the group they belong to. They internalise group norms, values, and ways of seeing the world. The more strongly one identifies with a group, the more group membership shapes behaviour and self-evaluation. This explains why group threats (insults to one's group) feel personally threatening.

**3. Social Comparison**
To evaluate their group — and thereby their own social identity — people compare their in-group to relevant out-groups on valued dimensions. When comparison favours the in-group, social identity is positive, supporting self-esteem. When comparison is unfavourable, individuals employ strategies: leave the group, individually achieve (psychological mobility), redefine the comparison dimension, or collectively contest the status hierarchy (social change).

**Minimal Group Paradigm:**
Tajfel's experiments divided participants into groups on trivial, arbitrary bases (preferences for Klee vs Kandinsky paintings, or random assignment). Participants showed strong in-group favouritism in allocating rewards — even sacrificing absolute gains to maximise the difference between in-group and out-group (relative favouritism). This demonstrated that categorisation alone — without prior conflict, history, or interaction — is sufficient to produce discrimination.

**Social Identity and Everyday Life:**
Social identity theory explains many phenomena beyond laboratory settings: nationalism and patriotism, brand loyalty (identifying with Apple vs PC users), caste and communal identities in India, and fashion. Fashion choices signal group membership and social identity — wearing particular brands, styles, or symbols communicates group belonging and social distinction. Turner's self-categorisation theory (an extension) explains how situational context shifts the salient level of identity (personal, social, or human).

In conclusion, social identity theory reveals that much of intergroup behaviour — including prejudice, discrimination, and in-group loyalty — is driven by the fundamental human need for positive social identity and self-esteem.

## 📝 Short Note: Social Identity (Section C — ~100 words)

Social identity refers to the part of one's self-concept derived from group membership. Tajfel and Turner's social identity theory proposes three processes: social categorisation (classifying self and others into groups), social identification (adopting the group's identity), and social comparison (evaluating one's group against out-groups to achieve positive distinctiveness). The minimal group paradigm demonstrated that even arbitrary groupings produce in-group favouritism. Social identity underlies nationalism, religious loyalty, caste identity, and brand preferences — groups central to self-concept are defended vigorously.
