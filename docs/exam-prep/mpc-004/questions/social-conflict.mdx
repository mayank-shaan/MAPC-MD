## 🔥 Most Repeated Question: Nature and Forms of Social Conflict and Methods of Conflict Resolution (12 appearances)

### Answer (Section A — ~450 words):

Social conflict is a state of tension or struggle between two or more parties (individuals, groups, or organisations) who perceive incompatible goals, scarce resources, or interference from others in achieving their objectives. Conflict is ubiquitous in social life and can be both destructive (reducing cooperation, increasing hostility) and constructive (driving innovation, clarifying values, promoting social change).

**Nature of Social Conflict:**
Conflict involves perceived incompatibility — actual incompatibility is not necessary; the perception of it is sufficient. It involves interdependence (conflicts arise between parties who affect each other), emotion (particularly fear, anger, or resentment), and communication breakdown. Conflict exists on a continuum from latent (underlying tension not yet expressed) to manifest (open, active struggle).

**Forms/Types of Social Conflict:**

*Intrapersonal Conflict*: Conflict within a single individual — competing desires, values, or roles. Lewin identified approach-approach (two positive goals), avoidance-avoidance (two negative options), and approach-avoidance (same goal has positive and negative aspects) conflicts.

*Interpersonal Conflict*: Conflict between two or more individuals. Common in close relationships, workplaces, and community settings. Triggered by resource competition, value differences, or personality clashes.

*Intergroup Conflict*: Conflict between social groups — racial, religious, political, or class-based. Realistic group conflict theory (Sherif) attributes intergroup conflict to competition for scarce resources. Social identity theory adds that even without resource competition, categorisation into in-groups and out-groups produces conflict.

*Interorganisational Conflict*: Conflict between organisations competing for markets, resources, or influence.

*Social Conflicts in India*: Caste conflict, communal (Hindu-Muslim) conflict, class conflict, and regional/linguistic conflicts are prominent forms of social conflict in the Indian context.

**Methods of Conflict Resolution:**

*Negotiation*: Direct communication between conflicting parties to reach a mutually acceptable agreement. Interest-based negotiation (Fisher and Ury's principled negotiation) focuses on underlying interests rather than stated positions, seeking integrative (win-win) solutions. BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement) determines each party's minimum acceptable outcome.

*Mediation*: A neutral third party facilitates communication and helps identify solutions, but has no authority to impose a settlement. Mediation preserves relationships and is used in family, workplace, and community disputes.

*Arbitration*: A neutral third party (arbitrator) hears both sides and imposes a binding decision. Faster and less costly than litigation; preserves some flexibility over adjudication.

*Adjudication (Litigation)*: Formal legal proceedings in which a judge or tribunal imposes a decision. Appropriate for serious legal violations but costly, time-consuming, and adversarial.

*Superordinate Goals*: Creating shared goals that require cooperation (Sherif's Robbers Cave) transforms conflicting parties into collaborators, reducing hostility.

In conclusion, social conflict is managed most effectively when parties shift from positional bargaining to interest-based problem-solving, supported by skilled third-party intervention when needed.

## 📝 Other Questions

### Blake-Mouton Two-Dimensional Model of Conflict Handling (Section B — ~250 words)

Blake and Mouton (1964) proposed a grid model of conflict management based on two independent dimensions: concern for self/own outcomes and concern for others/relationship. Their combination produces five conflict-handling styles:

**1. Competing (High self, Low other)**
Pursuing one's own goals at others' expense. Assertive and uncooperative. Appropriate when quick, decisive action is needed (emergencies) or when defending important principles. Risk: damages relationships and trust.

**2. Accommodating (Low self, High other)**
Yielding to others' wishes, neglecting own interests. Cooperative but unassertive. Appropriate when the issue matters more to the other party, or when preserving the relationship is paramount. Risk: own important concerns go unaddressed.

**3. Avoiding (Low self, Low other)**
Withdrawing from the conflict entirely. Neither assertive nor cooperative. Appropriate for trivial issues or when emotions run too high for productive discussion. Risk: conflict festers and worsens.

**4. Collaborating (High self, High other)**
Seeking a win-win solution that fully satisfies both parties. Both assertive and cooperative. Most appropriate when both parties' concerns are important and a creative integrative solution is possible. Most time-consuming but produces the most durable resolutions.

**5. Compromising (Moderate self, Moderate other)**
Splitting the difference — both parties give up something to reach a quick, intermediate resolution. Appropriate when goals are moderately important and time is limited. Risk: neither party is fully satisfied.

Effective managers are flexible — choosing the appropriate style based on the situation, stakes, and relationship importance.
