## 🌟 Comprehensive Answer (~500 words)

### Introduction
**Charles Spearman** (1904, 1927) proposed the **Two-Factor Theory of Intelligence**, one of the earliest and most enduring theories in the psychometric tradition. Using a statistical technique he developed — **factor analysis** — Spearman found that performance across diverse cognitive tests was positively correlated, suggesting a common underlying factor. He named this general factor **'g'** (general intelligence) and proposed that each specific test also measures a unique **'s'** factor (specific intelligence).

### The Two Factors

**1. The g-factor (General Intelligence)**
- A single, pervasive mental energy present to varying degrees in all individuals
- Common to all intellectual tasks — whenever you do any intellectual task, g is involved
- Highly heritable; relatively stable across the lifespan
- Spearman described it as **"mental energy"** — the capacity for abstract reasoning, the ability to see relationships and correlations between ideas
- Measured indirectly through diverse cognitive tests; the variance common to all tests estimates g

**2. The s-factor (Specific Intelligence)**
- Unique to each specific task — reading has its own s, arithmetic has its own s, spatial visualization has its own s
- Not shared across tasks
- Lower heritability than g; more influenced by specific training and experience

### Spearman's Method: Tetrad Differences

Spearman used the **method of tetrad differences** to identify g. If all correlations between tests can be explained by a single common factor, then any 2×2 submatrix of the correlation matrix should have zero tetrad differences. This statistical criterion was Spearman's original validation method — later replaced by modern confirmatory factor analysis.

### Evidence Supporting the g-Factor
1. **Positive manifold**: All cognitive ability tests correlate positively with each other — consistent with a common underlying factor
2. **Heritability**: Twin studies show g is highly heritable (estimated 50–80% in adults)
3. **Neural correlates**: "Neural efficiency hypothesis" — individuals with higher g show less brain activation on cognitive tasks (more efficient neural processing)
4. **Predictive validity**: g predicts academic achievement, job performance, and other real-world outcomes better than specific abilities alone

### Critical Appraisal / Limitations

**Strengths:**
1. First systematic, quantitative theory of intelligence using factor analysis
2. The g-factor has remarkable predictive validity across domains
3. Positive manifold (all test intercorrelations being positive) is a robust empirical finding

**Criticisms:**
1. **Thurstone's challenge**: L.L. Thurstone (1938) used factor analysis differently and identified **7 Primary Mental Abilities (PMA)** — Verbal Comprehension, Word Fluency, Number Facility, Spatial Visualization, Associative Memory, Perceptual Speed, and Induction. He found little evidence for a general factor. (However, second-order factor analysis of Thurstone's abilities does yield a g-like factor.)

2. **Oversimplification**: Intelligence is far more complex than a single number. Gardner and Sternberg both proposed theories recognizing multiple dimensions.

3. **Circular reasoning**: g is defined by the tests used — different test batteries yield different factors.

4. **Cultural bias**: Factor analysis of intelligence tests conducted in Western populations may not generalize cross-culturally.

5. **g as statistical artifact**: Critics argue g may be a statistical artifact of positively correlated tests rather than a real psychological or neural entity.

### Spearman vs Thurstone

| Aspect | Spearman | Thurstone |
|--------|----------|-----------|
| Theory | Two factors: g + s | 7 Primary Mental Abilities |
| Method | Tetrad differences | Centroid factor analysis |
| Number of factors | 1 general | 7 specific |
| View on g | Real mental energy | Emergent second-order factor only |
| Legacy | IQ tests preserve g as core | Multifactorial ability profiles |

### Conclusion
Spearman's two-factor theory was a foundational contribution to intelligence research. The g-factor remains one of the most replicated findings in psychology — its predictive validity is beyond doubt. However, Spearman's claim that g is the only factor of importance has been challenged by evidence for specific abilities, multiple intelligences, and the need for multidimensional assessment.
