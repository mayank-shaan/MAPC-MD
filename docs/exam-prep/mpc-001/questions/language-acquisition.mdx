## 🌟 Comprehensive Answer: Behaviouristic vs Innateness Theory (~500 words)

### Introduction

Language acquisition — the process by which children develop their first language — has been explained by two major competing theoretical frameworks: the **behaviouristic** (learning) approach and the **nativist** (innateness) approach. These theories represent a fundamental debate between **nurture** (language is learned) and **nature** (language capacity is innate). The behaviourist account was dominant in the 1950s until Chomsky's devastating critique transformed the field.

---

### Behaviouristic Theory (Skinner, 1957)

**B.F. Skinner** in *Verbal Behavior* (1957) argued that language is acquired through the same mechanisms as any other behaviour — **operant conditioning**:

1. **Imitation**: Children imitate sounds, words, and sentences they hear from caregivers. Imitation provides the raw material for language learning.

2. **Reinforcement**: Correct vocalizations (approximations to real words) are **positively reinforced** by caregivers through praise, attention, and getting what they want. Incorrect forms are extinguished through lack of reinforcement or correction.

3. **Verbal operants**: Skinner identified specific types of verbal behavior:
   - *Mands*: requests ("Milk!" → gets milk = reinforced)
   - *Tacts*: labeling ("Dog!" when a dog appears = social reinforcement)
   - *Echoics*: imitation of sounds
   - *Intraverbals*: verbal responses to verbal stimuli

**Strengths**: Explains why children learn the language of their environment; accounts for individual variation in vocabulary.

**Weaknesses (Chomsky's critique, 1959)**:
- Children produce sentences they have **never heard** ("I goed to school") — imitation cannot account for novel utterances.
- **Language acquisition is too rapid** — children acquire complex grammar by age 5 with relatively little explicit correction.
- **Poverty of the stimulus**: The input children receive is insufficient (incomplete sentences, false starts, unfamiliar vocabulary) to account for the grammatical competence they acquire.
- Parents rarely correct **grammar** systematically — they correct facts, not structure ("No sweetie, say it correctly" is rare).

---

### Innateness / Nativist Theory (Chomsky, 1957, 1965)

**Noam Chomsky** proposed that humans are biologically prepared for language acquisition:

**1. Language Acquisition Device (LAD)**
An innate, species-specific cognitive structure that enables children to extract the grammatical rules of any human language from the input they receive. The LAD contains **Universal Grammar** — the abstract grammatical principles common to all human languages.

**2. Universal Grammar (UG)**
All human languages share deep structural principles (e.g., all have nouns and verbs, all have ways of asking questions, all have recursion). This universality suggests a shared biological basis.

**3. Critical Period Hypothesis**
Language acquisition is most natural during a **critical/sensitive period** (birth to puberty), after which learning a language to native-like proficiency becomes much more difficult. Evidence: Genie (the "wild child" discovered at age 13) could not acquire full grammatical competence despite intensive instruction.

**4. Poverty of the Stimulus Argument**
The input available to children is insufficient to explain the rich grammatical knowledge they acquire — therefore, grammatical principles must be **pre-programmed** in the LAD.

**Strengths**:
- Explains the universality of language and its rapid, effortless acquisition
- Accounts for children's ability to generate novel, grammatically correct sentences
- Consistent with neuropsychological evidence (Broca's area for language production)

**Weaknesses**:
- The LAD remains a theoretical construct — its precise neurological basis is not fully identified
- Does not fully explain how children learn the specific vocabulary and phonology of their language
- Overstates innateness; social interaction and input quality clearly matter (Tomasello's social-pragmatic approach)

### Stages of Language Acquisition

Children universally pass through these stages:

| Stage | Age | Features |
|-------|-----|---------|
| **Cooing** | 0–2 months | Vowel-like sounds |
| **Babbling** | 4–6 months | Consonant-vowel combinations (ba-ba, ma-ma) |
| **Holophrastic** | 10–13 months | Single words carry full meaning ("Milk" = "I want milk") |
| **Two-word** | 18–24 months | Telegraphic speech — content words only ("Daddy go", "More juice") |
| **Telegraphic** | 2–3 years | Short sentences, grammatical function words emerging |
| **Multiword** | 3+ years | Longer, complex sentences; overgeneralization errors (goed, foots) |
| **Mature grammar** | 5+ years | Near-adult competence in syntax |

**Overgeneralization errors** (e.g., "I goed home", "He runned") are particularly important — children apply grammatical rules too broadly, demonstrating that they have extracted rules rather than just memorized forms. This is strong evidence against pure imitation/behaviourist accounts.

### Comparison Table

| Aspect | Behaviouristic (Skinner) | Innateness (Chomsky) |
|--------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| Mechanism | Imitation + reinforcement | LAD + Universal Grammar |
| Language is | Learned behavior | Biologically programmed |
| Input needed | Extensive, reinforced input | Impoverished input sufficient |
| Child's role | Passive recipient | Active hypothesis tester |
| Universality | Due to similar environments | Due to shared LAD |
| Novel sentences | Cannot explain | Explained by UG + LAD |
| Weakness | Cannot explain novelty | LAD neurologically vague |

---

### Conclusion

The debate between behaviourist and nativist accounts has been productive — driving research into the mechanisms of language acquisition from both biological and environmental perspectives. Contemporary **interactionist** accounts (e.g., Tomasello's social-pragmatic approach) recognize that both innate capacities and rich social input are necessary for full language development. Neither position alone is sufficient.
