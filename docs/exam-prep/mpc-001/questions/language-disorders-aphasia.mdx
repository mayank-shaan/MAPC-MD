## 🌟 Comprehensive Answer: Language Disorders & Aphasia (~500 words)

### Introduction

**Aphasia** is an acquired language disorder resulting from **brain damage** — most commonly caused by stroke, head injury, or tumours — that affects a person's ability to speak, understand, read, or write. It does not affect intelligence but disrupts the mechanisms of language processing. The study of aphasia has been central to understanding the **neurological organisation of language**, particularly the roles of specific brain regions.

---

### Neurological Basis: Language Areas

Language is predominantly processed in the **left hemisphere** (in about 95% of right-handed people). Two key areas:

- **Broca's area** (Brodmann area 44-45): Left inferior frontal gyrus — speech **production** and grammatical processing
- **Wernicke's area** (Brodmann area 22): Left superior temporal gyrus — speech **comprehension** and language processing
- **Arcuate fasciculus**: White matter tract connecting Broca's and Wernicke's areas

---

### Types of Aphasia

#### 1. Broca's Aphasia (Expressive/Non-fluent Aphasia)

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Location** | Broca's area (left frontal lobe) |
| **Speech** | Non-fluent, effortful, telegraphic ("want...cup...milk") |
| **Comprehension** | Relatively preserved |
| **Repetition** | Impaired |
| **Named after** | Paul Broca (1861) — patient "Tan" |

Patients **know what they want to say** but struggle to produce it. Grammar words ("the," "is") are often omitted.

---

#### 2. Wernicke's Aphasia (Receptive/Fluent Aphasia)

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Location** | Wernicke's area (left temporal lobe) |
| **Speech** | Fluent but meaningless — **jargon aphasia** |
| **Comprehension** | Severely impaired |
| **Repetition** | Impaired |
| **Named after** | Carl Wernicke (1874) |

Patients produce long strings of words but with **paraphasias** (wrong words or invented words). They often do not realise their speech is meaningless.

---

#### 3. Conduction Aphasia

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Location** | Arcuate fasciculus (connecting Broca's and Wernicke's) |
| **Speech** | Fluent |
| **Comprehension** | Good |
| **Repetition** | Severely impaired — disproportionate deficit |

The key feature is **impaired repetition** despite preserved comprehension and relatively fluent speech.

---

#### 4. Global Aphasia

Severe damage to both Broca's and Wernicke's areas; affects **all modalities** — speaking, understanding, reading, writing. Most severe form.

#### 5. Anomic Aphasia

Word-finding difficulty (**anomia**) — unable to retrieve names of objects. Fluent speech with frequent pauses and circumlocution ("the thing you write with").

#### 6. Transcortical Aphasias (Motor and Sensory)

Similar to Broca's and Wernicke's but **repetition is preserved** — lesions outside the classical language areas but disconnecting them.

---

### Comparative Summary

| Type | Fluency | Comprehension | Repetition |
|------|---------|---------------|------------|
| Broca's | Non-fluent | Good | Poor |
| Wernicke's | Fluent | Poor | Poor |
| Conduction | Fluent | Good | Very poor |
| Anomic | Fluent | Good | Good |
| Global | Non-fluent | Poor | Poor |

---

### Conclusion

Aphasia research has profoundly shaped our understanding of the neural substrates of language. The classical Broca-Wernicke model, while simplified, remains the foundation. Modern neuroimaging has revealed that language is processed in a broader network, but the core distinction between expressive (Broca's) and receptive (Wernicke's) aphasia remains clinically and theoretically valuable. Understanding aphasia types guides diagnosis, treatment, and our understanding of language and the brain.

## 🧠 Memory Aid
**"Broca = Broken speech; Wernicke = Weird speech"**  
- Broca: Can't say it right but **understands**
- Wernicke: Speaks fluently but it's **nonsense**

**Conduction**: "Can't **Con**duct (repeat) even though both ends work"
