## 🌟 Comprehensive Answer: History and Meaning of Cognitive Psychology (~500 words)

### Introduction

**Cognitive psychology** is the scientific study of mental processes — including perception, attention, memory, language, reasoning, problem solving, and decision making. It seeks to understand how people acquire, store, process, and use information. The term was popularized by **Ulric Neisser** in his landmark 1967 text *Cognitive Psychology*, which is often considered the founding document of the modern field.

---

### Pre-history: Structuralism and Introspection

The roots of cognitive psychology lie in 19th-century **experimental psychology**. **Wilhelm Wundt** (1879) founded the first psychological laboratory in Leipzig and used **introspection** — systematic self-observation — to study conscious experience. He aimed to identify the basic elements ("structure") of mental life — a school of thought called **Structuralism**.

**Edward Titchener** brought Structuralism to America and further developed introspection as a method. However, introspection faced fundamental problems: it was subjective, unreliable, and difficult to verify independently — making it unscientific by the standards of natural science.

---

### The Behaviourist Era (1913–1950s)

**John B. Watson** (1913) declared that psychology should abandon the study of the mind entirely and focus exclusively on observable **behaviour**. His "behaviourist manifesto" argued that mental processes were unobservable and therefore unscientific. **B.F. Skinner** extended this with **operant conditioning**, explaining all behaviour in terms of stimulus-response reinforcement contingencies.

During the behaviourist era (approximately 1913–1950s), mental processes were effectively **banned** from scientific psychology. Yet some researchers quietly maintained interest in internal processes:

- **Edward Tolman** (1948) demonstrated **cognitive maps** in rats — showing that even rodents formed internal representations of their environment, not mere stimulus-response chains.
- **Gestalt psychologists** (Wertheimer, Köhler, Koffka) studied perception and insight, arguing that the whole is more than the sum of its parts.

---

### The Cognitive Revolution (1950s–1960s)

A cluster of events in the mid-1950s catalyzed the **cognitive revolution** — the return of mind to scientific psychology:

1. **George Miller (1956)**: Published "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two," demonstrating that short-term memory has a limited capacity of ~7 items. This was an explicitly mentalistic finding — it described a property of the mind, not just behaviour.

2. **Noam Chomsky (1957/1959)**: Published *Syntactic Structures* and his devastating review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior*, arguing that language is too complex and too rapid in acquisition to be explained by reinforcement. He proposed an innate **Language Acquisition Device (LAD)** — an internal cognitive mechanism.

3. **Newell & Simon (1956)**: Presented the **Logic Theorist**, one of the first computer programs to simulate human reasoning. This established the **computer as a metaphor** for the mind — information processing.

4. **Donald Broadbent (1958)**: Published *Perception and Communication*, introducing an explicit **information processing model** of attention with a limited-capacity bottleneck — the first major cognitive model.

5. **Ulric Neisser (1967)**: Published *Cognitive Psychology*, providing the field with its name, framework, and scientific identity.

---

### Definition of Cognitive Psychology

Drawing these threads together, cognitive psychology can be defined as:

> "The scientific study of how people acquire, store, transform, and use information to guide thought and behaviour." (Neisser, 1967; Sternberg, 2009)

It is characterized by:
- **Mentalism**: Belief that internal mental processes are scientifically legitimate and important objects of study
- **Information processing approach**: The mind processes information analogously to a computer — input, processing, storage, output
- **Experimental methodology**: Controlled experiments, reaction time studies, and cognitive tasks to study mental processes
- **Cognitive neuroscience link**: Modern cognitive psychology is deeply connected with brain imaging and neuropsychology

---

### Domains of Cognitive Psychology

Cognitive psychology encompasses:
- **Perception** — how we interpret sensory information
- **Attention** — selective focus on relevant information
- **Memory** — encoding, storage, and retrieval
- **Language** — comprehension, production, acquisition
- **Thinking** — reasoning, problem solving, decision making
- **Intelligence** — individual differences in cognitive ability
- **Consciousness** — self-awareness and metacognition

---

### Conclusion

Cognitive psychology emerged from the failure of behaviourism to account for the richness of human mental life. The cognitive revolution restored the mind to psychology — not through the subjectivism of introspection, but through rigorous experimental methods and the powerful metaphor of the computer. Today, cognitive psychology is the dominant theoretical framework in psychology, underpinning clinical psychology, educational psychology, human-computer interaction, and cognitive neuroscience.

## 📝 Section B Answer: Key Issues in Cognitive Psychology (~300 words)

*For 2017-December, 2018-December, 2021-December*

Cognitive psychology grapples with several enduring theoretical controversies:

**1. Nature vs Nurture**: How much of cognitive ability is genetically determined vs shaped by experience and environment? This debate is particularly prominent in intelligence (Spearman's g vs environmental factors), language acquisition (Chomsky's innate LAD vs Skinner's learning), and perceptual development.

**2. Modularity vs General Processing**: Fodor (1983) proposed that the mind consists of **domain-specific modules** — specialized, automatic, informationally encapsulated systems (e.g., face recognition, language). Alternatively, **general process theorists** argue for domain-general mechanisms (working memory, attention) that apply across all cognitive tasks. Neuropsychological evidence supports some modularity (double dissociations), but general processes clearly also exist.

**3. Symbolic vs Connectionist Processing**: Classical cognitive science assumed the mind manipulates discrete symbols according to rules (like a computer). **Connectionism / PDP models** (Rumelhart & McClelland, 1986) propose that cognition emerges from patterns of activation across neural-like networks — no explicit symbols, no explicit rules. Both approaches have empirical support.

**4. Ecological Validity**: Laboratory experiments in cognitive psychology often use artificial tasks (word lists, nonsense syllables, dot patterns). **Ecological validity** questions whether these findings generalize to real-world cognition. Gibson's ecological approach argued that cognition should be studied in real environments.

**5. Reductionism**: Can cognitive processes be fully explained by neural mechanisms, or do they require their own level of description? Cognitive neuroscience attempts to bridge both levels.

These issues remain unresolved and actively drive research, making cognitive psychology a dynamic, evolving discipline.
