## 🌟 Comprehensive Answer (~500 words)

### Introduction

The scientific measurement of intelligence began in late 19th century France. The history of intelligence testing is marked by increasing sophistication — from simple sensory tests to standardized batteries assessing multiple cognitive abilities. This history is inseparably linked with **Alfred Binet**, who created the first practical intelligence test for identifying children needing educational support.

---

### Alfred Binet — Father of Intelligence Testing

**Alfred Binet** (1857–1911), along with Théodore Simon, developed the **Binet-Simon Scale** in 1905 for the French Ministry of Education. The goal was practical — to identify children who could not benefit from regular schooling and needed special education.

**Key contributions:**

**1. The 1905 Binet-Simon Scale**
- 30 tasks arranged in increasing difficulty
- Ranged from following a lighted match with the eyes (easy) to defining abstract concepts (difficult)
- Assessed practical judgment and problem solving, not just sensory ability
- Deliberately atheoretical — Binet wanted a practical tool, not a theory of intelligence

**2. The 1908 Revision — Mental Age (MA)**
- Introduced the concept of **Mental Age (MA)** — the age at which a child's performance is typical
- A 10-year-old who performs at the level of an average 12-year-old has a Mental Age of 12
- Tests grouped by age level; each age level contained tests passed by 75%+ of children that age

**3. The 1911 Revision**
- Extended to adult levels; 5 tests per age level
- Binet died the same year, before his ideas were fully developed

**4. IQ — William Stern (1912)**
- German psychologist William Stern introduced the **Intelligence Quotient (IQ)**:
  **IQ = (Mental Age / Chronological Age) × 100**
- A child with MA = CA has IQ = 100 (average)
- A child with MA > CA has IQ > 100 (above average)

---

### Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scale

**Lewis Terman** at Stanford University revised Binet's scale for American children — creating the **Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scale** (1916). Key features:
- Adopted Stern's IQ formula
- Extended norms to American populations
- Subsequent revisions: 1937, 1960, 1972, 1986, 2003 (Stanford-Binet 5 — SB5)
- Modern SB5 measures five factors: Fluid Reasoning, Knowledge, Quantitative Reasoning, Visual-Spatial Processing, Working Memory — in both verbal and nonverbal formats

---

### Wechsler Intelligence Scales

**David Wechsler** criticized the Stanford-Binet's heavy reliance on language and single mental age score. He developed separate scales for different age groups:

- **WAIS** (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale) — adults 16+ years
- **WISC** (Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children) — ages 6–16
- **WPPSI** (Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale) — ages 2½–7

**Key innovations:**
1. **Deviation IQ**: Instead of MA/CA ratio, Wechsler used the **deviation IQ** — expressing performance as standard deviations from the mean (M=100, SD=15). This solved the problem of ratio IQ becoming meaningless in adults.
2. **Verbal and Performance scales**: Wechsler separated verbal tasks (vocabulary, comprehension, similarities) from performance/non-verbal tasks (block design, picture arrangement) — providing a more comprehensive profile.
3. **Full Scale IQ**: Combined score from verbal and performance subscales.

**Modern WAIS-IV/WAIS-5 structure** (four index scores):
- Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI)
- Perceptual Reasoning Index (PRI)
- Working Memory Index (WMI)
- Processing Speed Index (PSI)

### Brief History of Intelligence Measurement

| Year | Contribution |
|------|-------------|
| 1869 | Galton — hereditary genius; sensory measures of intelligence |
| 1890 | Cattell — "mental tests" for sensory capacities |
| 1905 | Binet & Simon — first practical intelligence scale (France) |
| 1908 | Binet — mental age concept |
| 1912 | Stern — IQ formula |
| 1916 | Terman — Stanford-Binet (USA) |
| 1917 | Army Alpha & Beta — mass testing in WWI |
| 1939 | Wechsler-Bellevue Scale — adult intelligence |
| 1949 | WISC — children's scale |
| 1972 | Das, Kirby, Jarman — PASS theory |
| 1983 | Gardner — Multiple Intelligences |
| 1985 | Sternberg — Triarchic Theory |
| 1997 | Das & Naglieri — CAS (PASS-based test) |

### Conclusion

The measurement of intelligence has evolved from Binet's practical test to sophisticated, multi-factorial batteries. The shift from MA/CA ratio IQ to deviation IQ, from single-score to profile-based assessment, and from purely verbal to nonverbal measures reflects growing understanding of intelligence's complexity.
