## 🌟 Comprehensive Answer: Problem Solving — Definition & Types (~500 words)

### Introduction

**Problem solving** is one of the most fundamental and extensively studied domains of cognitive psychology. It refers to the **cognitive process of moving from a current state to a desired goal state** when the path is not immediately obvious. Problem solving involves recognising that a problem exists, understanding its nature, generating possible solutions, evaluating them, and implementing the best solution. It is central to human intelligence and adaptive behaviour.

---

### Definition of a Problem

A problem exists when there is:
1. A **current (initial) state** — the present situation
2. A **goal state** — the desired outcome
3. **Obstacles** — barriers preventing direct movement from initial to goal state
4. **Operators** — actions available to transform states

**Newell and Simon (1972)** formalised this as the **Problem Space** — the set of all possible states that could exist between the initial state and the goal state, plus the operators that move between states.

---

### Well-Defined vs Ill-Defined Problems

| Type | Characteristics | Examples |
|------|----------------|---------|
| **Well-defined** | Clear initial state, clear goal, clear operators | Solving a maths equation, chess |
| **Ill-defined** | Vague initial or goal state, unclear constraints | Writing a poem, designing a building, life decisions |

Most real-world problems are **ill-defined** — they require the problem solver to first **define** the problem before solving it.

---

### Types of Problems

**Greeno (1978)** classified problems into three main types:

#### 1. Problems of Arrangement
Rearranging elements to satisfy some criterion:
- **Anagrams**: Rearranging letters to form a word (e.g., RCBKI → BRICK)
- **String problems**: Attaching strings in a room (Maier, 1931)
- Often solved through **trial-and-error** or insight

#### 2. Problems of Inducing Structure
Identifying the relationship between elements and extending it:
- **Analogy problems**: A:B :: C:? (e.g., Doctor:Hospital :: Teacher:?)
- **Series completion**: 2, 4, 6, 8, ?
- Require identifying the underlying **rule or pattern**

#### 3. Problems of Transformation
Changing an initial state into a goal state through a sequence of moves/operations:
- **Tower of Hanoi**: Move discs from one peg to another following rules
- **Hobbits & Orcs (missionaries & cannibals)**: Move characters across a river
- Require planning sequences of operations

---

### Additional Classification

| Category | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Routine problems** | Previously practised; solved by applying known procedures |
| **Non-routine problems** | Require novel approaches; no known algorithm |
| **Insight problems** | Solved through sudden restructuring (Gestalt "Aha!" experience) |
| **Convergent problems** | One correct answer (e.g., maths) |
| **Divergent problems** | Multiple possible answers (e.g., brainstorming) |

---

### Insight vs Non-Insight Problems

**Gestalt psychologists** (Wertheimer, Köhler, Duncker) highlighted **insight** — the sudden realisation of a solution through **perceptual restructuring** of the problem. Köhler's famous experiments with Sultan the chimpanzee showed insight in animals. Insight problems often involve overcoming **functional fixedness** (inability to see novel uses for familiar objects).

---

### Conclusion

Understanding the definition and types of problems is foundational to studying problem solving. Problems vary enormously — from well-defined mathematical puzzles to ill-defined real-world challenges — and different types require different cognitive strategies. The problem-space framework of Newell and Simon provides a unifying representation, while Greeno's classification identifies the specific demands of arrangement, induction, and transformation tasks.
