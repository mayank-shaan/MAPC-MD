## 📝 Master Answer — Section A (450 words / 10 marks)

### Q. Describe Sullivan's theory of personality. (10 marks)

*Also: "Discuss Sullivan's theory of personality." (2024-Jun); "Describe Sullivan's developmental epochs." (2023-Jun B6)*

**Introduction**

Harry Stack Sullivan (1892–1949) developed an **interpersonal theory of personality**, arguing that personality cannot be studied in isolation — it exists only in the context of interpersonal relationships. He defined personality as "the relatively enduring pattern of recurrent interpersonal situations which characterise a human life." Personality is thus not a fixed internal structure but a pattern of relating to others.

**Key Concepts**

**1. Dynamisms**
A *dynamism* is a relatively enduring energy transformation — the smallest unit of personality. Dynamisms are expressed in interpersonal situations:
- *Lust dynamism*: Genital zone drive activated in adolescence
- *Self-system dynamism*: Most important — organisation of experience aimed at avoiding anxiety and maintaining security in interpersonal relations

**2. Personifications**
Personifications are organised clusters of feelings, attitudes, and concepts about oneself and others derived from interpersonal experience:
- *Good-me*: Aspects of self associated with reward and approval — integrated into the self-system
- *Bad-me*: Aspects of self associated with anxiety and mild disapproval — partly integrated
- *Not-me*: Aspects of self associated with intense anxiety and dissociation — excluded from conscious awareness
- *Good-mother / Bad-mother*: Early interpersonal images of the caregiver

**3. The Self-System**
The self-system develops to manage anxiety arising from interpersonal relationships. It uses **security operations** (analogous to defense mechanisms) to minimise anxiety: *selective inattention* (ignoring anxiety-provoking experiences), *sublimation*, and *dissociation*.

**4. Anxiety and Security**
Anxiety arises from anticipated disapproval in interpersonal situations. The fundamental human motivation is to avoid anxiety and achieve **interpersonal security**. The pursuit of security often conflicts with the satisfaction of other needs.

**Developmental Epochs**

Sullivan proposed six stages of personality development, each defined by characteristic interpersonal relationships:

| Epoch | Age | Key Interpersonal Experience |
|-------|-----|------------------------------|
| Infancy | Birth–2 years | Oral zone; empathic linkage with mothering figure; development of personifications |
| Childhood | 2–5 years | Language acquisition; imaginative play; acculturation |
| Juvenile Era | 5–8 years | First experience of peers; competition, cooperation, ostracism; social accommodation |
| Preadolescence | 8–12 years | Chumship — close same-sex friendship; genuine intimacy and collaboration |
| Early Adolescence | Puberty–15 | Lust dynamism activated; integration of intimacy and lust needs |
| Late Adolescence | 15–adult | Establishment of stable interpersonal patterns; mature integration of needs |

**Critical Evaluation**

Sullivan's emphasis on the social origins of personality was ahead of its time and influenced object relations theory and attachment theory. However, his idiosyncratic terminology (dynamisms, personifications) makes his theory difficult to communicate, and it lacks systematic empirical testing.

**Conclusion**

Sullivan's interpersonal theory offers a relational, social account of personality development — a significant departure from instinct-based psychoanalytic models. His concept of the self-system and developmental epochs provide a clinically useful framework for understanding how interpersonal experiences shape personality.

- **Developmental epochs** are the most frequently tested component — memorise all six with ages and key interpersonal features.
- The distinction between **good-me / bad-me / not-me** is a common Section B point.
- Sullivan's definition of personality ("relatively enduring pattern of recurrent interpersonal situations") is worth quoting directly.
