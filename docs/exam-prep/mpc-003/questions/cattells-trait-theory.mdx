## 📝 Master Answer — Section A (450 words / 10 marks)

### Q. Explain Cattell's formula for personality. Describe the categories of traits stated by him. (10 marks)

*Also: "Discuss Cattell's trait approach to personality." (2020-Dec); "Explain Cattell's trait theory of personality." (2023-Dec)*

**Introduction**

Raymond B. Cattell (1905–1998) applied factor analysis rigorously to personality data to uncover the fundamental dimensions of personality. His goal was to move personality psychology from armchair theorising to empirical science. Cattell defined personality as "that which permits a prediction of what a person will do in a given situation," captured in his famous formula:

**R = f (S, P)**

Where **R** = response (behaviour), **S** = stimulus situation, and **P** = personality. This formula emphasises that behaviour is a function of both the person and the situation — a precursor to interactionist models.

**Data Sources**

Cattell collected personality data from three sources:
1. **L-data (Life record data)**: Ratings of behaviour in real-life situations by observers
2. **Q-data (Questionnaire data)**: Self-report questionnaires measuring how individuals perceive themselves
3. **T-data (Objective test data)**: Responses to standardised tasks in which the subject is unaware of what is being measured

**Categories of Traits**

Cattell classified traits along several dimensions:

**1. Surface Traits vs. Source Traits**
- *Surface traits*: Observable clusters of behaviours that appear to go together (e.g., sociability, talkativeness). These are the "surface" expressions.
- *Source traits*: The underlying, unitary factors that determine surface traits. Identified through factor analysis, they are the true building blocks of personality.

**2. Common Traits vs. Unique Traits**
- *Common traits*: Dimensions shared across all individuals in a culture (e.g., intelligence, dominance) — allow comparison between people
- *Unique traits*: Traits specific to a particular individual, not shared with others

**3. Ability, Temperament, and Dynamic Traits**
- *Ability traits*: Skills that determine how effectively a person achieves goals (e.g., intelligence, creativity)
- *Temperament traits*: Constitutional, stylistic aspects of behaviour (e.g., speed, energy, emotional reactivity)
- *Dynamic traits*: Motivational aspects — drives, attitudes, and interests. Subdivided into **Ergs** (inborn drives, e.g., sex, hunger, fear) and **Sentiments** (learned patterns of interest)

**4. Constitutional vs. Environmental-Mould Traits**
- *Constitutional traits*: Originate from biological endowment
- *Environmental-mould traits*: Shaped by experience and environment

**The 16 Primary Factor Personality Questionnaire (16PF)**

Through factor analysis of L, Q, and T data, Cattell identified **16 primary source traits** (e.g., A: Reserved vs Outgoing; B: Less Intelligent vs More Intelligent; C: Emotionally Unstable vs Stable; H: Shy vs Bold; Q3: Undisciplined vs Self-disciplined). These are measured by the **16PF questionnaire**, widely used in clinical, occupational, and educational settings. Second-order factor analysis of the 16PF yielded broader factors including **Exvia–Invia** (similar to Extraversion) and **Anxiety**.

**Conclusion**

Cattell's contribution lies in bringing statistical rigour to trait psychology. By distinguishing surface from source traits and providing the 16PF as a measurement tool, he transformed personality assessment from intuition to empirical science. His work laid the groundwork for the later Big Five model.

## 💡 Exam Tips

- The **surface vs source trait** distinction is the most important — always explain it clearly.
- "Cattell's formula" (R = f(S, P)) is a quick mark-scorer — always include it in Section A.
- In Section B questions asking only about "categories of traits," skip the formula and data sources to stay within 250 words.
- Cattell and Allport are frequently contrasted — know the key difference: Allport was idiographic, Cattell was nomothetic and statistical.
