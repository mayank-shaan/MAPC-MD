## 📝 Master Answer — Section A (450 words / 10 marks)

### Q. Elucidate Carl Rogers' theory of personality. (10 marks)

*Also: "Critically evaluate the Carl Rogers' theory of personality." (2020-Dec); "Explain Rogers' theory of Personality." (2022-Jun)*

**Introduction**

Carl Rogers (1902–1987) developed a person-centred theory of personality rooted in clinical observation and humanistic philosophy. Unlike Freud who saw humans as driven by unconscious instincts, Rogers believed all humans possess an innate **actualising tendency** — a drive toward growth, wholeness, and the realisation of one's potential. His theory centres on the self-concept and the conditions that promote or hinder healthy personality development.

**The Phenomenological Field**

Rogers argued that each person lives in a unique subjective world — the **phenomenological field** — which is their private reality. What matters is not the objective world but how the person perceives and interprets their experience. The totality of a person's experience at any moment constitutes their phenomenal field; the portion of this that the person comes to identify as "I" or "me" forms the **self**.

**Self-Concept: Real Self and Ideal Self**

- **Real Self (Self-Concept)**: How a person currently perceives themselves — their actual characteristics, values, and attributes
- **Ideal Self**: How a person would like to be — the self they aspire toward

Healthy personality involves **congruence** — a close match between real self and ideal self. When a significant gap exists between them, the person experiences **incongruence** — anxiety, defensiveness, and psychological distress.

**Actualising Tendency**

All organisms have an innate drive toward growth, maintenance, and enhancement — the actualising tendency. In humans, this expresses specifically as the self-actualising tendency: the drive to maintain and enhance the self-concept. This positive force is always present but can be blocked by environmental conditions.

**Conditions of Worth**

In childhood, we receive **positive regard** (love, approval) from significant others — parents and caregivers. When positive regard is made conditional — "I will love you IF you behave in certain ways" — the child develops **conditions of worth**: internalised beliefs about what one must be or do to deserve love and approval. To maintain positive regard, the child distorts or denies experiences that conflict with conditions of worth, creating incongruence between the organismic self (true experience) and the self-concept.

**Unconditional Positive Regard**

The antidote to conditions of worth is **unconditional positive regard** — acceptance and love without conditions. When provided by significant others or a therapist, it allows the person to drop defensive distortions and move toward congruence and self-actualisation.

**The Fully Functioning Person**

Rogers described the psychologically healthy individual — the fully functioning person — as characterised by:
1. Openness to experience (no defensive denial)
2. Existential living (living fully in each moment)
3. Organismic trusting (trusting one's own feelings and reactions)
4. Experiential freedom (sense of free will and choice)
5. Creativity (flexible adaptation to changing circumstances)

**Critical Evaluation**

*Strengths*: Optimistic, humanistic; revolutionised psychotherapy (client-centred therapy); validated subjective experience; empirically supported in counselling research.  
*Weaknesses*: Overly optimistic about human nature; neglects unconscious processes and social-structural constraints; self-actualisation is difficult to operationalise; culturally biased toward Western individualism.

**Conclusion**

Rogers' person-centred theory offers a profoundly optimistic account of personality — one that locates dysfunction in social conditions rather than instinctual drives, and that prescribes growth through authentic relationship and unconditional acceptance. Its influence on psychotherapy, counselling, and education has been immense.

## 💡 Exam Tips

- Rogers is often asked alongside Maslow as "Humanistic Theory" — know the comparison: both emphasise growth; Maslow uses a needs hierarchy; Rogers focuses on self-concept and therapeutic conditions.
- **Incongruence** is the central pathological concept — always explain it clearly.
- For "Critical Evaluation" questions, balance strengths (optimism, client-centred therapy) with weaknesses (cultural bias, untestable concepts).
