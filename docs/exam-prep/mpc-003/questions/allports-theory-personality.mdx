## 📝 Master Answer — Section A (450 words / 10 marks)

### Q. Discuss in detail the trait approach proposed by Allport. (10 marks)

*Also: "Elucidate the concepts of functional autonomy and mature personality as given by Allport." (2024-Jun, 2018-Dec, 2017-Jun)*

**Introduction**

Gordon Allport (1897–1967) is regarded as the father of trait psychology. He defined personality as "the dynamic organisation within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine his characteristic behaviour and thought." Allport placed traits at the centre of personality, arguing that they are real neuropsychic units that guide behaviour consistently across situations and time.

**Allport's Concept of Traits**

A **trait** is a generalised and focused neuropsychic system (peculiar to the individual) with the capacity to render many stimuli functionally equivalent and to initiate and guide consistent forms of adaptive and expressive behaviour. Allport identified eight key characteristics of traits: they are real (not theoretical), they are more general than habits, they are dynamic and motivating, their existence can be established empirically, they are only relatively independent of other traits, a trait is not synonymous with moral or social judgement, acts consistent with a trait can be viewed in light of the trait, and traits can be viewed from both the idiographic and nomothetic perspective.

**Personal Dispositions (Types of Traits)**

Allport distinguished three levels of personal dispositions:
1. **Cardinal traits**: The most pervasive, dominant dispositions that virtually define an individual's entire existence. Few people have a cardinal trait (e.g., Mother Teresa's selflessness). Named after historical figures — "Machiavellian," "Quixotic."
2. **Central traits**: The 5–10 core traits that characterise an individual and are easily noted by others. These are the traits you would mention in a letter of recommendation.
3. **Secondary traits**: More peripheral, less consistent dispositions that appear in specific situations (e.g., food preferences, situational anxiety).

**Proprium (Development of the Self)**

Allport used the term **proprium** to describe the self or ego — the warm, central core of personality. He outlined 8 developmental stages of the proprium:
1. Bodily self (0–1 year)
2. Self-identity (1–2 years)
3. Self-esteem / pride (2–3 years)
4. Extension of self (4–6 years)
5. Self-image (4–6 years)
6. Self as rational coper (6–12 years)
7. Propriate striving (adolescence)
8. The knower / self as the knower (adulthood)

**Functional Autonomy**

One of Allport's most original contributions is the concept of **functional autonomy** — the idea that adult motives are independent of the original motives that gave rise to them. Behaviour that once served a biological need becomes an end in itself. For example, a person who initially played violin for parental approval may continue playing as an adult purely for the intrinsic pleasure it brings. Allport identified two types: **perseverative functional autonomy** (habitual, repetitive behaviours maintained by feedback loops) and **propriate functional autonomy** (higher-level motives that have become central to one's selfhood and values).

**Psychologically Mature Adult**

Allport described the fully developed, mature personality through seven criteria: extension of sense of self, warm relating to others, emotional security and self-acceptance, realistic perception and skills, self-objectification (insight and humour), a unifying philosophy of life, and propriate striving (engagement with meaningful goals).

**Conclusion**

Allport's trait theory offers a richly idiographic perspective on personality. By emphasising the uniqueness of individual trait configurations, the developmental nature of the self (proprium), and the motivational independence of adult behaviour (functional autonomy), Allport moved personality psychology beyond both behaviourism and psychoanalysis toward a genuinely humanistic science.

## 📝 Section B Answer (250 words / 6 marks)

### Q. Elucidate the concepts of functional autonomy and mature personality as given by Allport. (6 marks)

**Functional Autonomy**

Functional autonomy refers to the principle that adult motives become independent of the childhood or biological needs that originally motivated them. Behaviours initially undertaken for survival or reward acquire their own motivational status. The carpenter who began working for wages may continue carpentry in retirement for its own sake — the motive has become autonomous.

Two types:
- **Perseverative functional autonomy**: Lower-level, maintained through neurological feedback (e.g., continued drug use, compulsive habits)
- **Propriate functional autonomy**: Higher-level, linked to self-image, values, and long-term goals — the most characteristically human form

**Psychologically Mature Adult**

Allport's seven criteria for psychological maturity:
1. *Extension of sense of self* — involvement in activities and relationships beyond the self
2. *Warm human relationships* — capacity for intimacy and compassion
3. *Emotional security and self-acceptance* — tolerating frustration without excessive anxiety or defensiveness
4. *Realistic perception and problem-centred coping* — seeing the world accurately without distortion
5. *Self-insight and humour* — ability to see oneself objectively and laugh at oneself
6. *Unifying philosophy of life* — a coherent set of values, beliefs, or religion that gives life direction
7. *Propriate striving* — long-term, self-consistent goals that give meaning and purpose

Together, these criteria describe a person who is engaged, secure, realistic, and purposeful — a stark contrast to the defensive, driven person of Freudian theory.
