## 📝 Master Answer — Section A (450 words / 10 marks)

### Q. Define personality. Highlight the role of biological and psychological factors in personality development. (10 marks)

*Also: "Discuss the biological and psychological factors that influence the development of personality." (2019-Dec, 2018-Dec); "Define personality. Explain the role of psychological factors in personality development." (2024-Dec)*

**Introduction and Definition**

The term **personality** derives from the Latin *persona* (the mask worn by actors), but modern usage refers to the consistent patterns of thought, emotion, and behaviour that characterise an individual. Several influential definitions highlight different facets:

- **Allport (1937)**: "Personality is the dynamic organisation within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine his characteristic behaviour and thought."
- **Eysenck (1953)**: "Personality is the more or less stable and enduring organisation of a person's character, temperament, intellect, and physique."
- **Cattell (1950)**: "Personality is that which permits a prediction of what a person will do in a given situation."

Common threads across definitions: personality is (a) organised/structured, (b) consistent across time and situations, (c) unique to the individual, and (d) expressed in behaviour.

**Biological Determinants of Personality**

1. **Heredity**: Twin studies (especially identical twins reared apart) demonstrate significant genetic contributions to personality traits. Bouchard et al. (1990) found ~50% heritability for Big Five traits. Temperamental differences observable in infancy (activity level, emotionality, sociability) are largely genetically determined.

2. **Body Build and Physique**: Sheldon's somatotype theory linked body build to temperament: ectomorphs (cerebrotonia), mesomorphs (somatotonia), endomorphs (viscerotonia). Though largely discredited in its strong form, body image and physical health still influence self-concept.

3. **Nervous System Functioning**: Eysenck argued that extraversion is determined by cortical arousal levels — introverts have higher baseline arousal and seek stimulation reduction; extraverts have lower arousal and seek stimulation. Neuroticism is linked to limbic system reactivity.

4. **Endocrine System**: Hormones such as testosterone (aggression, dominance), cortisol (stress reactivity), and serotonin (mood regulation) influence personality characteristics. Hormonal imbalances can alter personality functioning.

5. **Brain Structures**: Frontal lobe integrity is linked to impulse control and social behaviour. Limbic system function relates to emotional reactivity. Neurological conditions (e.g., frontal lobe damage) can profoundly alter personality.

**Psychological Determinants of Personality**

1. **Self-Concept**: How individuals perceive themselves — their abilities, worth, and identity — shapes their motivations, interpersonal style, and coping strategies. Rogers emphasized congruence between real and ideal self as central to healthy personality.

2. **Motivation and Needs**: Internal drives, conscious goals, and unconscious needs shape personality expression. Maslow's hierarchy illustrates how unsatisfied needs dominate personality and behaviour.

3. **Cognitive Processes**: Perceptual styles, attributional tendencies, and cognitive schemas influence how individuals interpret events and respond to them. Beck's cognitive schemas and Kelly's personal constructs are examples.

4. **Emotional Development**: Early attachment experiences (Bowlby, Ainsworth) shape internal working models that influence adult emotional regulation and relationship patterns.

5. **Unconscious Processes**: Freud's psychoanalytic theory highlights how unconscious conflicts, defence mechanisms, and unresolved childhood experiences shape adult personality.

**Interaction Principle**

No single determinant operates in isolation. Biological predispositions (temperament) interact with early experiences (parenting, trauma) and cultural context to produce the unique personality. Diathesis-stress models suggest biological vulnerabilities are expressed only under certain environmental conditions.

**Conclusion**

Personality development is the product of a complex interplay between biological endowment and psychological experience. Understanding both sets of determinants is essential for a comprehensive account of personality, and for effective clinical intervention.
