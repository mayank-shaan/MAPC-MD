## Key Question: Assumptions, Types and Steps in Ethnography

### Answer (Section A — ~450 words):

Ethnography is a qualitative research method originating in cultural anthropology that involves extended, first-hand immersion in a social setting to understand the culture, practices, and meanings of participants from an insider (emic) perspective. The term derives from Greek: ethnos (people/culture) + graphia (writing).

**Assumptions of Ethnography:**
Culture is shared meaning — people in a social group share understandings about the world that guide their behaviour. These meanings are not directly observable but are expressed in language, artefacts, and practices. Ethnography assumes that understanding requires prolonged engagement in the natural setting rather than brief snapshots; that the researcher's subjectivity is a resource (not merely a bias) for understanding; and that social life is best understood holistically, in context.

**Types of Ethnography:**

*1. Realist Ethnography*: The researcher presents an objective, third-person account, minimising their own presence in the text. Claims authority through "being there." Traditional anthropological style.

*2. Confessional Ethnography*: Researcher's own experiences, struggles, and perspectives in the field are foregrounded. Acknowledges researcher's role in shaping the account.

*3. Critical Ethnography*: Combines ethnographic methods with critical theory to expose and challenge power relations, inequality, and marginalisation within the setting studied.

*4. Virtual Ethnography (Netnography)*: Adapted for online communities and digital spaces. Observes and participates in online forums, social media, and virtual communities.

**Steps in Ethnographic Research:**

1. *Select a site and gain access*: Identify a community or setting; negotiate entry through gatekeepers; build trust.
2. *Participant observation*: The primary data collection method. The researcher participates in group activities while simultaneously observing. Degree of participation ranges from complete participation to complete observer.
3. *Field notes*: Detailed records made as soon as possible after observation. Include descriptive notes (what happened) and analytic memos (interpretive reflections).
4. *Interviews*: Key informant interviews and informal conversations supplement observation. Provide insider perspectives and clarify observations.
5. *Document analysis*: Examine existing records, artefacts, and texts within the setting.
6. *Data analysis*: Identify patterns, themes, and cultural categories through iterative reading and coding. Produce thick description.
7. *Writing the ethnography*: Present findings as narrative. Balance description and interpretation; use participants' voices.

**Strengths**: Ecological validity — behaviour studied in natural context. Depth — reveals tacit knowledge and cultural meanings invisible to surveys. Holistic understanding.

**Limitations**: Time-consuming; difficult to replicate; researcher bias (going native — losing analytic distance); ethical issues (covert observation, informed consent in natural settings); limited generalisability.
