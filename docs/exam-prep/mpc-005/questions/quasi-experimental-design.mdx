## 🔥 Most Repeated Question: Types of Quasi-Experimental Design / vs True Experimental (12 appearances)

### Answer (Section A — ~450 words):

A quasi-experimental design resembles a true experimental design but lacks full experimental control — most critically, random assignment of participants to conditions. The term "quasi" (Latin: as if) signals that these designs approximate experiments without meeting all their criteria. They are widely used when random assignment is unethical, impractical, or impossible.

**Distinction from True Experimental Design:**

| Feature | True Experimental | Quasi-Experimental |
|---------|------------------|-------------------|
| Random assignment | Yes | No |
| Manipulation of IV | Yes | Yes |
| Control group | Yes | Sometimes |
| Internal validity | High | Moderate (more threats) |
| Ethical feasibility | Sometimes limited | Often higher |
| Real-world applicability | Lower | Higher |

**Types of Quasi-Experimental Design:**

*1. Non-Equivalent Control Group Design*
Two pre-existing groups are compared — one receives the treatment, the other serves as control — but participants were not randomly assigned to groups. Pre-tests are administered to assess baseline equivalence.

*Design*: O₁ X O₂ (experimental group)
         O₃   O₄ (control group, no X)

*Threat*: Selection bias — groups may differ at baseline on important characteristics. Pre-test comparison helps identify but cannot eliminate this threat.

*2. Interrupted Time-Series Design*
Multiple observations are made over time before and after an intervention. The researcher looks for a discontinuity — a change in level or slope — at the point of intervention.

*Design*: O₁ O₂ O₃ O₄ X O₅ O₆ O₇ O₈

*Advantages*: Controls for maturation and regression threats (because the trend before intervention can be projected). Useful for evaluating policy interventions (e.g., the effect of a new law on traffic fatalities).
*Threat*: History — any event occurring at the time of intervention could explain the discontinuity.

*3. Regression-Discontinuity Design*
Participants are assigned to treatment based on a cutoff score on a continuous assignment variable. Those above/below the cutoff receive the treatment; the discontinuity at the cutoff is the treatment effect.

*Example*: Students scoring below 50 on an entrance test receive remedial education; the researcher looks for a discontinuity in outcomes at the cutoff.

*Advantage*: Assignment rule is known and modelled, making causal inference relatively strong for a non-experimental design.

*4. Separate Sample Pre-test Post-test Design*
Different (non-equivalent) samples are measured at pre-test and post-test. Less powerful than repeated measurement but avoids testing effects.

**Advantages of Quasi-Experimental Designs:**
Greater ecological validity; feasible when randomisation is impossible; often the only option in applied policy and programme evaluation research.

**Limitations:**
Multiple threats to internal validity; cannot definitively establish causality; requires more statistical sophistication to handle pre-existing group differences.

## 📝 Other Questions

### Advantages and disadvantages of quasi-experimental design (Section B — ~250 words)

**Advantages:**
*Feasibility*: When random assignment is impractical (e.g., studying effects of natural disasters, government policies, or clinical interventions), quasi-experiments are the best available option.
*Ethical appropriateness*: Cannot withhold beneficial treatments from a control group — quasi-experimental designs allow study without this ethical violation.
*External validity*: Studies are conducted in real-world settings, increasing generalisability compared to laboratory experiments.
*Longitudinal feasibility*: Time-series designs track natural change over time.

**Disadvantages:**
*Weak internal validity*: Selection bias is the primary threat — non-equivalent groups may differ on unmeasured variables.
*Confounding*: Without random assignment, it is difficult to rule out alternative explanations for observed effects.
*Interpretation difficulty*: Multiple plausible explanations for findings; statistical corrections (matching, ANCOVA) are imperfect.
*Regression to mean*: Particularly problematic when groups are selected on extreme scores.
