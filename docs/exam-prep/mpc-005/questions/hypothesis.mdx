## 🔥 Key Question: Types of Hypothesis and Difficulties in Formulation (Section B)

### Answer (~250 words):

**Definition:**
A hypothesis is a tentative, testable proposition about the expected relationship between two or more variables. It translates the research question into a specific, predictive statement that can be empirically evaluated.

**Types of Hypothesis:**

| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| Research (H₁) | Directional or non-directional prediction | "Cognitive therapy reduces anxiety more than medication" |
| Null (H₀) | States no relationship or no difference | "There is no difference in anxiety reduction between therapy types" |
| Directional | Predicts specific direction of effect | "Group A will score higher than Group B" |
| Non-directional | Predicts difference without specifying direction | "There will be a difference between Group A and Group B" |
| Alternative | Any hypothesis other than the null | Equivalent to research hypothesis in most contexts |

**Characteristics of a Good Hypothesis:**
1. *Testable* — capable of empirical verification.
2. *Falsifiable* — can be proven wrong by evidence.
3. *Precise* — clearly identifies variables and expected relationship.
4. *Parsimonious* — simple; avoids unnecessary complexity.
5. *Consistent with theory* — grounded in existing knowledge.
6. *Limited scope* — focused enough to be testable.

**Difficulties in Formulating Hypothesis:**
1. *Insufficient knowledge of existing literature* — without adequate review, hypotheses may be redundant or theoretically unsound.
2. *Lack of conceptual clarity* — vague constructs cannot yield precise predictions.
3. *Ethical constraints* — some relationships cannot be studied directly (e.g., effects of abuse).
4. *Complexity of psychological phenomena* — multiple interacting variables make single-variable predictions difficult.
5. *Absence of good measurement tools* — if a construct cannot be reliably measured, hypotheses about it cannot be tested.

**Errors in Hypothesis Testing:**

| Error Type | Definition | Consequence |
|-----------|------------|-------------|
| Type I (α) | Reject null when it is true (false positive) | Accepting an ineffective treatment |
| Type II (β) | Fail to reject null when it is false (false negative) | Missing a real effect |

Controlling Type I error is set by α level (usually .05); reducing Type II error requires adequate sample size (statistical power).
