## Key Question: Correlational Design — Types, Advantages and Limitations

### Answer (Section A — ~450 words):

Correlational research design is a non-experimental design that examines the relationship between two or more variables as they naturally occur, without manipulation of any variable. The researcher measures variables and analyses the degree and direction of their association using correlation coefficients.

**Purpose:**
To determine whether and how strongly variables are related; to predict scores on one variable from scores on another; to establish the direction of relationship (positive or negative); to inform subsequent experimental or explanatory research.

**Types of Correlational Design:**

*1. Bivariate Correlational Design*
Examines the relationship between two variables. The Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient (r) measures the strength and direction of linear relationship between two continuous variables. Ranges from -1.0 (perfect negative) to +1.0 (perfect positive); 0 = no linear relationship. Spearman's rho is used for ranked/ordinal data.

*2. Multivariate Correlational Design*
Examines relationships among three or more variables simultaneously. Includes:
- *Multiple regression*: Predicts one criterion variable from multiple predictors.
- *Factor analysis*: Identifies underlying factors among a set of intercorrelated variables.
- *Canonical correlation*: Examines relationships between sets of variables.
- *Structural equation modelling (SEM)*: Tests complex causal models involving latent variables.

*3. Path Analysis*
A form of multiple regression that tests causal models specifying directional relationships among variables. Does not require experimental manipulation; tests the plausibility of proposed causal pathways. Cannot establish causality but can rule out implausible models.

**Advantages:**
Feasible when manipulation is impossible or unethical (e.g., studying genetic variables, childhood experiences). Can examine relationships in naturalistic settings. Allows prediction. Provides the foundation for more powerful multivariate analyses.

**Limitations:**
*Cannot establish causality*: Correlation ≠ causation. Three possible explanations for A-B correlation: A causes B; B causes A; a third variable C causes both. Only experimental manipulation with random assignment can eliminate all alternative explanations.
*Third-variable problem*: Unmeasured variables may explain observed correlations (e.g., ice cream sales and drowning deaths are both correlated with summer temperature, not causally related to each other).
*Directionality problem*: The direction of causation cannot be determined from a correlation alone.
*Linearity assumption*: Pearson r only captures linear relationships; non-linear relationships may be missed.
*Restriction of range*: If the range of one variable is restricted (e.g., measuring IQ in a gifted sample), the correlation will be underestimated.

**Standards of Quality:**
Adequate reliability of measures; sufficient sample size for statistical power; checks for outliers (which inflate or deflate correlations); consideration of non-linearity; confidence intervals around r.
