## 🔥 Most Repeated Question: Concept, Characteristics & Steps (Section A)

### Answer (~450 words):

**Definition:**
Ex-post facto research (Latin: "after the fact") is a systematic empirical inquiry in which the researcher cannot directly control the independent variable because it has already occurred, or because it is inherently not manipulable. The researcher identifies the effect (DV) first and then looks back to identify potential causes (IV). It is also called **causal-comparative research** (though some authors distinguish the two) or retrospective research.

Classic example: comparing academic achievement of students raised in high-literacy vs low-literacy home environments — the researcher cannot randomly assign children to these environments, so the "cause" is studied after it has naturally occurred.

**Characteristics:**

1. *No manipulation of IV*: The independent variable has already occurred. The researcher measures it as it exists in nature.
2. *Retrospective in nature*: Investigation proceeds from observed effect back to its probable causes.
3. *No random assignment*: Participants cannot be randomly assigned to conditions, which limits causal inference.
4. *Naturally occurring groups*: Groups are pre-formed on the basis of the IV (e.g., smokers vs non-smokers; males vs females).
5. *Limited control*: The researcher cannot rule out alternative explanations with the certainty that experimental control provides.
6. *Correlational logic*: Even though differences between groups are examined, causality is inferred, not demonstrated.

**Steps in Ex-Post Facto Research:**

1. *Define the problem*: Identify the phenomenon to be studied and the specific research question.
2. *Review the literature*: Examine existing research to understand the variables and potential causal pathways.
3. *Formulate hypotheses*: State the expected relationships between IV and DV. Since IV cannot be manipulated, hypotheses are about existing group differences.
4. *Identify and select participants*: Select groups that differ on the IV of interest (criterion-group selection). Sample should be representative.
5. *Identify and operationalise variables*: Define the dependent variable and select or develop appropriate measures.
6. *Collect data*: Administer measures to all participants. Often uses surveys, tests, records, or observation.
7. *Analyse data*: Use appropriate statistical procedures — typically t-tests, ANOVA, chi-square, or regression depending on variable type.
8. *Interpret results*: Interpret differences between groups cautiously — avoid asserting causation from correlation. Consider alternative explanations.
9. *Report findings*: Present results, limitations, and implications.

**Strengths:**
- Allows study of variables that cannot ethically or practically be manipulated (e.g., child abuse, natural disaster experience).
- Yields data in natural settings, enhancing ecological validity.
- Economical — does not require creating experimental conditions.

**Limitations:**
- Cannot establish definitive causal relationships.
- Subject to confounding variables — groups may differ on variables other than the IV.
- Direction of causality is ambiguous.
- Retrospective data may be incomplete or inaccurate.

**Comparison with Experimental Research:**

| Dimension | Experimental | Ex-Post Facto |
|-----------|-------------|--------------|
| IV manipulation | Yes — researcher controls IV | No — IV has already occurred |
| Random assignment | Yes | No |
| Causal inference | Strong | Weak — inferential only |
| Control | High | Limited |
| Settings | Lab or controlled field | Natural |
| Ethical constraints | Some topics cannot be manipulated | Studies ethically sensitive variables |
