## 🔥 Most Repeated Question: Types of Validity and Threats to Internal Validity (11 appearances)

### Answer (Section A — ~450 words):

Validity refers to the degree to which a research study measures what it claims to measure or, in the context of experimental design, whether the conclusions drawn from a study are justified. Cook and Campbell identified four types of validity relevant to research design.

**Types of Validity:**

*Internal Validity*: The degree to which changes in the dependent variable can be attributed to the independent variable rather than to confounding factors. Internal validity is the sine qua non of experimental research — without it, causal inferences are unwarranted.

*External Validity*: The degree to which findings can be generalised to other people, settings, times, and operationalisations. A study can be internally valid but not generalisable if it used a highly unrepresentative sample or artificial setting.

*Construct Validity*: The degree to which the operationalisation of variables accurately represents the theoretical constructs they are intended to measure. Poor construct validity means the study is testing something different from what it claims.

*Statistical Conclusion Validity*: The degree to which the statistical procedures used are appropriate and the conclusions about covariation are correct. Violations include low statistical power, violated statistical assumptions, and fishing for significance.

**Threats to Internal Validity (Campbell and Stanley, 1963):**

1. *History*: Extraneous events occurring between pre-test and post-test that affect the DV. Controlled by a comparison group.
2. *Maturation*: Participants change over time (grow older, become fatigued) independently of the IV. Controlled by time-limited studies or matched controls.
3. *Testing*: Exposure to a pre-test sensitises participants and affects post-test scores. Controlled by a Solomon four-group design.
4. *Instrumentation*: Changes in measuring instruments or observers over time. Controlled by standardised measurement.
5. *Statistical Regression (Regression to the Mean)*: Groups selected on extreme scores tend to score closer to the mean on retesting regardless of treatment. Controlled by not selecting extreme groups or using randomisation.
6. *Selection Bias*: Pre-existing differences between groups due to non-random assignment. Controlled by random assignment.
7. *Experimental Mortality (Attrition)*: Differential dropout of participants from groups. Controlled by examining who drops out and why.
8. *Selection-Maturation Interaction*: Different groups mature at different rates. Particularly problematic in quasi-experimental designs.

**Threats to External Validity:**
*Population validity*: Whether the sample represents the population. *Ecological validity*: Whether the laboratory setting represents natural settings. *Temporal validity*: Whether findings hold across different time periods. *Reactive effects*: Participants behave differently knowing they are being studied (Hawthorne effect).

In conclusion, internal validity is essential for causal inference; external validity determines the scope of generalisation. Both must be considered in research design, though they often trade off — tightly controlled laboratory studies maximise internal validity at the cost of external validity.

## 📝 Other Questions

### Differentiate internal and external validity (Section B — ~250 words)

| Dimension | Internal Validity | External Validity |
|-----------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Definition | Causal relationship between IV and DV is unambiguous | Findings generalise beyond the study context |
| Question | "Did X cause Y in this study?" | "Do these findings apply elsewhere?" |
| Threats | History, maturation, testing, instrumentation, regression, selection, mortality | Sampling bias, reactive effects, setting specificity |
| Enhanced by | Random assignment, control groups, standardisation | Random sampling, multiple settings, replication |
| Trade-off | High internal validity often requires artificial conditions | Naturalistic settings reduce control |

A study with high internal validity but low external validity establishes a causal relationship in a narrow, artificial context that may not generalise. Ideally both are high, but researchers must be explicit about which is prioritised and why.
