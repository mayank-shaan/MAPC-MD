## 🔥 Most Repeated Question: Nature, Criteria and Types of Case Study (Section A/B)

### Answer (~450 words):

**Definition and Nature:**
A case study is an in-depth, intensive investigation of a single individual, group, event, organisation, or phenomenon within its real-life context. Introduced in psychology by Freud, Janet, and Piaget, it remains a cornerstone of clinical, developmental, and social psychology. The unit of analysis can be a person, family, community, institution, or critical incident.

Yin (2009) defines it as "an empirical inquiry that investigates a contemporary phenomenon in depth and within its real-life context, especially when the boundaries between phenomenon and context are not clearly evident."

**Key Characteristics:**
- Focuses on a single unit (case) with the goal of understanding it holistically.
- Uses multiple sources of data — interviews, observations, documents, artefacts.
- Is bounded in time and activity.
- Produces rich, thick descriptions that retain context.
- Can be exploratory, descriptive, or explanatory in purpose.

**Types of Case Studies:**

*1. Intrinsic Case Study*
Undertaken because the case itself is of primary interest — its uniqueness or typicality is the reason for study. No attempt to generalise; the case has intrinsic value.

*2. Instrumental Case Study*
The case is used as an instrument to understand a broader issue or refine a theory. The case is of secondary interest; it facilitates understanding of something else.

*3. Collective/Multiple Case Study*
Several cases are studied jointly to understand a phenomenon, population, or general condition. Allows comparison and cross-case analysis.

*4. Exploratory Case Study*
Used when the research questions are not clearly defined; helps identify research questions and hypotheses for future study.

*5. Explanatory Case Study*
Seeks to explain causal links in real-life interventions. Example: Why did a particular therapy succeed with this client but fail with another?

**Steps in Case Study Research:**

1. *Select the case*: Identify the case that best addresses the research question. Purposive selection.
2. *Build a theoretical framework*: Develop the conceptual framework that will guide data collection and analysis.
3. *Select data sources*: Identify interviews, documents, observations, and records to be used.
4. *Collect data*: Gather data from multiple sources (triangulation) to build a comprehensive picture.
5. *Analyse data*: Identify patterns and themes within and across data sources. Pattern matching and explanation building are common strategies.
6. *Interpret and write up*: Develop a case narrative; relate to theory; state conclusions with appropriate caution.

**Strengths:**
- Preserves holistic and contextual features of real-life events.
- Enables study of rare phenomena (e.g., split-brain patients, unique clinical conditions).
- Generates hypotheses for future research.
- Flexible and responsive to emerging findings.

**Limitations:**
- Cannot yield statistical generalisation.
- Prone to researcher bias in data selection and interpretation.
- Time-consuming; labour-intensive.
- Replication is difficult.

**Common Misconceptions (Yin):**
1. Case studies cannot be used to generalise — **False**: case studies support *analytical* (theoretical) generalisation, not statistical generalisation.
2. Case studies are only exploratory — **False**: they can be explanatory and descriptive too.
3. Case studies lack rigour — **False**: rigorous case studies follow clear protocols and use triangulation.

## 📝 Short Note Version (Section C — ~120 words)

A case study is an intensive, in-depth investigation of a single unit (person, group, event) within its real-life context using multiple data sources. Types include intrinsic, instrumental, and collective case studies. Steps involve selecting the case, building a theoretical framework, collecting data from multiple sources (triangulation), analysing patterns, and writing the case narrative. Strengths include contextual richness and ability to study rare phenomena; limitations include inability to statistically generalise, susceptibility to researcher bias, and difficulty of replication. Yin (2009) distinguishes between statistical generalisation (not possible) and analytical generalisation (possible — applying findings to theory).
