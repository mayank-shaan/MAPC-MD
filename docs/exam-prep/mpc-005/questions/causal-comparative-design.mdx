## 🔥 Key Question: Causal-Comparative vs Correlational vs Experimental (Section B)

### Answer (~300 words):

**Definition:**
Causal-comparative research (also called ex-post facto in some frameworks) is a type of non-experimental research in which the researcher attempts to determine the cause or consequence of differences that already exist between groups. The researcher identifies a DV (effect) and looks back retrospectively to identify the probable IV (cause). No manipulation of variables is attempted.

Example: Comparing cognitive performance of children who attended preschool vs those who did not — the researcher did not assign children to these groups; the "treatment" has already occurred.

**Causal-Comparative vs Correlational Research:**

| Dimension | Causal-Comparative | Correlational |
|-----------|-------------------|--------------|
| Purpose | Determine cause-effect relationships | Determine degree of association |
| Groups | Two or more pre-formed groups | Single sample |
| Data type | Group differences on DV | Continuous scores on two or more variables |
| Analysis | t-test, ANOVA | Correlation coefficient, regression |
| Causal inference | Weak — retrospective | Not implied |
| Variable manipulation | None | None |

*Similarity*: Both are non-experimental; neither involves manipulation of IV; neither can definitively establish causality.

**Causal-Comparative vs Experimental Research:**

| Dimension | Causal-Comparative | Experimental |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------|
| IV manipulation | No | Yes |
| Random assignment | No | Yes |
| Control over extraneous variables | Limited | High |
| Causal inference | Weak | Strong |
| Group formation | Pre-existing | Researcher-created |
| Generalisability | Moderate | Varies |

*Similarity*: Both compare groups; both examine possible IV–DV relationships; both may use the same statistical analyses (t-test, ANOVA).

**Strengths of Causal-Comparative Design:**
- Studies variables that cannot be manipulated (e.g., gender, trauma history, SES).
- Economical — uses naturally occurring groups.
- Useful first step before experimental research.

**Limitations:**
- Cannot establish causality — direction of effect is ambiguous.
- Groups may differ on variables other than the IV of interest (confounding).
- Selection bias — groups were not randomly formed.

**Steps:**
1. Define the problem and identify groups.
2. Review literature and formulate hypothesis.
3. Select participants from existing groups.
4. Collect data on the DV.
5. Analyse for group differences.
6. Interpret cautiously — consider alternative explanations.
