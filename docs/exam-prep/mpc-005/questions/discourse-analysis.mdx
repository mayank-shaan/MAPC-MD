## 🔥 Most Repeated Question: Approaches and Steps of Discourse Analysis (12 appearances)

### Answer (Section A — ~450 words):

Discourse analysis is a qualitative research method that examines language as a social practice — how language is used to construct meanings, identities, and social realities in context. Unlike content analysis (which counts manifest features), discourse analysis focuses on how language functions and what it achieves.

**Assumptions of Discourse Analysis:**
Language is not a neutral vehicle for transmitting pre-formed thoughts; it actively constructs reality. Meaning is context-dependent and relational. Texts (spoken or written) perform social actions. There is no single, correct interpretation — multiple readings are possible. Discourse analysis rejects the idea of an objective, pre-linguistic reality that language merely represents.

**Approaches to Discourse Analysis:**

*1. Conversation Analysis (CA)*
Developed by Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson, CA examines naturally occurring talk in interaction. It focuses on turn-taking, repair sequences, and sequential organisation of conversation. CA is atheoretical — it avoids importing prior theoretical frameworks and treats talk as a topic in itself. Used to study medical consultations, courtroom interaction, and therapy sessions.

*2. Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA)*
Associated with Fairclough, Van Dijk, and Wodak, CDA examines how language reproduces and resists power relations and ideologies. It analyses texts at three levels: (a) the text itself (linguistic features), (b) discursive practice (how texts are produced and consumed), and (c) social practice (broader sociocultural context). CDA is applied to political speeches, media texts, and institutional language to reveal hidden ideologies.

*3. Foucauldian Discourse Analysis*
Based on Foucault's concept of discourse as a historically situated system of knowledge that defines what can be said and thought in a given period. Analyses how discourses constitute subjects and regulate behaviour. Used in psychology to analyse how clinical diagnoses (e.g., "madness") are socially constructed through professional discourse.

**Steps in Discourse Analysis:**

1. *Formulate research question*: Identify what aspects of language use or social practice you wish to understand.
2. *Select data*: Choose naturally occurring texts — interviews, documents, media, conversations. Sampling is purposive.
3. *Transcription*: Transcribe spoken data with conventions marking pauses, overlaps, intonation (especially for CA).
4. *Familiarise with data*: Read and re-read to identify patterns and regularities.
5. *Coding and analysis*: Identify discursive devices, rhetorical strategies, interpretive repertoires, and subject positions.
6. *Interpretation*: Relate patterns to theoretical framework — how does the discourse construct social realities or power relations?
7. *Write-up*: Present analysis with extensive data extracts; demonstrate how interpretations are grounded in the text.

**Relevance in Psychology:**
Discourse analysis challenges psychology's traditional focus on internal mental states by showing how psychological phenomena (emotions, attitudes, identities) are constructed through language and social interaction rather than simply expressed by it.

## 📝 Other Key Questions

### Distinguish discourse analysis from content analysis (Section B — ~250 words)

| Dimension | Discourse Analysis | Content Analysis |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Focus | How language constructs meaning and social reality | What is said; frequency of categories |
| Epistemology | Constructionist — meaning is context-dependent | Positivist — meaning is stable, countable |
| Data | Naturally occurring talk and texts | Any text; can include numerical coding |
| Analysis | Interpretive, holistic, attentive to context | Systematic categorisation, often quantified |
| Output | Thick description of discourse practices | Frequencies, statistical patterns |
| Reliability | Credibility through reflexivity and member-checking | Inter-rater reliability coefficients |

Discourse analysis is preferred when the research question is about *how* language functions; content analysis when the question is about *how much* or *how often* certain content appears.
