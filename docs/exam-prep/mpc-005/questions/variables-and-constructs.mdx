## 🔥 Most Repeated Question: Types of Variables (Section A/B)

### Answer (~450 words):

A **variable** is any characteristic, attribute, or quantity that can take on different values across individuals, situations, or time points. Variables are the observable, measurable units of research. A **construct**, by contrast, is an abstract, theoretical concept (e.g., intelligence, anxiety, motivation) that cannot be directly observed but is inferred from observable indicators. Constructs must be **operationalised** — translated into specific, measurable procedures — before they can function as variables in a study.

**Types of Variables:**

*1. Independent Variable (IV)*
The variable manipulated or selected by the researcher to observe its effect. In experimental research the IV is actively manipulated (e.g., type of teaching method); in non-experimental research it is the presumed causal variable (e.g., gender, SES). It is antecedent to the dependent variable.

*2. Dependent Variable (DV)*
The outcome variable that is measured to assess the effect of the IV. It is presumed to be influenced by the IV. Example: test scores (DV) following different teaching methods (IV).

*3. Extraneous Variable*
Any variable other than the IV that could influence the DV. If left uncontrolled, it introduces error into the results. Extraneous variables can be controlled by randomisation, matching, or holding them constant. Example: room temperature during a memory experiment.

*4. Confounding Variable*
An extraneous variable that systematically co-varies with the IV and thereby distorts the apparent IV–DV relationship. Unlike random error, confounding produces bias. Example: if students who receive a new teaching method also happen to be higher in motivation, motivation is a confound.

*5. Moderating Variable*
A variable that alters the direction or strength of the relationship between IV and DV. It interacts with the IV. Example: gender may moderate the effect of a stress-management programme on anxiety.

*6. Mediating Variable*
A variable that explains the mechanism or process through which the IV influences the DV. Example: self-efficacy may mediate the relationship between training and job performance.

**Types of Constructs:**

*1. Dispositional Constructs*: Stable traits attributed to individuals — e.g., introversion, intelligence, authoritarianism. Inferred from consistent patterns of behaviour.

*2. Stimulus–Response (S–R) Constructs*: Defined by the observable relationship between specific stimuli and responses — e.g., "hunger" defined as food-seeking behaviour after deprivation.

*3. Intervening Constructs*: Hypothetical internal states that mediate between stimulus and response — e.g., "drive," "cognitive map."

**Operationalisation:**
Converting a construct into a variable requires specifying how it will be measured. For example, "anxiety" (construct) may be operationalised as scores on the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory. Good operationalisation ensures validity (measuring what is intended) and reliability (consistency of measurement).

**Importance in Research:**
Clear identification and control of variables is foundational to research validity. Failure to distinguish confounding from independent variables is among the most common sources of invalid causal conclusions.

## 📝 Other Key Questions

### Extraneous and Confounding Variables — distinguish (Section B ~250 words)

| Dimension | Extraneous Variable | Confounding Variable |
|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Definition | Any uncontrolled variable that could affect DV | Extraneous variable that systematically varies with IV |
| Effect | Random error / noise | Systematic bias — distorts IV–DV relationship |
| Detection | Identified through experimental design | Detected through logical analysis or statistical control |
| Control | Randomisation, holding constant, matching | Randomisation, covariate analysis (ANCOVA) |
| Example | Room noise in a memory task | Pre-existing motivation in an educational study |

Confounding is more serious because it leads to incorrect causal conclusions, whereas extraneous variables increase error variance but do not bias estimates.
